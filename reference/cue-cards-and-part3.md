# Part 2 Cue Cards & Part 3 Escalation Trees

## Card Selection Rule

Pick the Cue Card **furthest** from what the candidate appeared most comfortable discussing in Part 1:
- If they leaned on tech/media or news in Part 1 -> pick **Set A (Ineffective Rule)** or **Set B (Decision Under Incomplete Information)**.
- If they leaned on corporate work processes or management -> pick **Set C (Time-Saving Tool That Added Work)** or **Set B**.
- Never let the candidate pick their own card.

---

## Set A: An Ineffective Rule or Procedure

**Official Handover Line:**
`"Now, I'm going to give you a topic, and I'd like you to talk about it for one to two minutes. Before you talk, you'll have one minute to think about what you're going to say. You can make some notes if you wish. Do you understand? Here is your paper and pencil, and here is your topic."`

> **Cue Card — Set A**
> Describe a rule or procedure at a workplace, school, or public institution that you think does not work well.
>
> You should say:
> - what the rule or procedure is
> - who has to follow it
> - why it fails to achieve what it was meant to do
> - and explain what happens when people try to work around it.

*(Rounding-off prompt if needed before Part 3: `"Do most people around you complain about that rule openly, or quietly ignore it?"`)*

### Part 3 Escalation Tree (Set A)
- **Tier 1 — Band 6 (Generalize & Compare):**
  - `"Why do large organizations often keep rules that employees clearly know are inefficient?"`
  - `"Is there a difference between how strict rules work in small companies versus large public bodies?"`
- **Tier 2 — Band 7 (Incentives & Causes):**
  - `"When a rule creates obvious extra effort without improving results, who usually benefits from keeping it on paper?"`
  - `"How does measuring staff compliance on paper change the way people actually do their jobs?"`
- **Tier 3 — Band 8–9 (Systemic Paradox & Counter-Argument):**
  - `"Some argue that eliminating bad rules simply replaces formal bureaucracy with informal office politics—that hidden power takes over when written procedures vanish. How far do you agree?"`
  - `"If every rule invites workarounds once people learn how it is enforced, can any large institution ever design genuinely self-correcting policies?"`

## Set B: A Decision Made With Incomplete Information

**Official Handover Line:**
`"Now, I'm going to give you a topic, and I'd like you to talk about it for one to two minutes. Before you talk, you'll have one minute to think about what you're going to say. You can make some notes if you wish. Do you understand? Here is your paper and pencil, and here is your topic."`

> **Cue Card — Set B**
> Describe an important decision you had to make when you did not have all the facts you wanted.
>
> You should say:
> - what the decision was
> - what information was missing at the time
> - how you went about making the choice anyway
> - and explain what that experience showed you about making decisions under uncertainty.

*(Rounding-off prompt if needed before Part 3: `"If you faced that same gap in information today, would you decide any faster?"`)*

### Part 3 Escalation Tree (Set B)
- **Tier 1 — Band 6 (Generalize & Compare):**
  - `"In general, do people today delay decisions more often than previous generations because they expect complete information first?"`
  - `"Why do some people rely heavily on advice from others while others trust their own judgment?"`
- **Tier 2 — Band 7 (Risk & Accountability):**
  - `"In professional settings, when does gathering more data stop improving the decision and start acting as a way to avoid blame?"`
  - `"How does the fear of making a visible mistake affect the kinds of risks institutions are willing to take?"`
- **Tier 3 — Band 8–9 (Paradox & Speculation):**
  - `"If leaders are punished more harshly for a transparent mistake made quickly than for a slow failure backed by mountains of data, what does that incentivize across an entire economy?"`
  - `"Is it realistic to expect human judgment to become better under uncertainty now that algorithmic forecasts exist, or does reliance on models make people less capable when data breaks down?"`

## Set C: A Tool That Promised to Save Time

**Official Handover Line:**
`"Now, I'm going to give you a topic, and I'd like you to talk about it for one to two minutes. Before you talk, you'll have one minute to think about what you're going to say. You can make some notes if you wish. Do you understand? Here is your paper and pencil, and here is your topic."`

> **Cue Card — Set C**
> Describe a tool, app, or piece of technology that was supposed to save time but ended up creating extra work.
>
> You should say:
> - what the tool or technology was
> - why you or your organization started using it
> - what unexpected work or problems it caused
> - and explain how people eventually dealt with the situation.

*(Rounding-off prompt if needed before Part 3: `"Would you say people still keep using that tool today out of habit?"`)*

### Part 3 Escalation Tree (Set C)
- **Tier 1 — Band 6 (Generalize & Compare):**
  - `"Why do people often adopt new tools before checking whether they actually fit the job?"`
  - `"Are older workers and younger workers affected differently when workplace tools change constantly?"`
- **Tier 2 — Band 7 (Economics & Hidden Costs):**
  - `"When software shifts work from a specialist onto everyone else—like self-service forms or automated trackers—why is that still sold as an efficiency gain?"`
  - `"What makes organizations reluctant to abandon a frustrating tool even after its hidden costs become obvious?"`
- **Tier 3 — Band 8–9 (Systemic Friction & Counter-Argument):**
  - `"If digital tools genuinely cut the cost of producing meetings, messages, and reports to near zero, does that increase real productivity or simply flood people with low-value obligations?"`
  - `"Who ought to carry the switching cost when a productivity platform breaks down—the vendor, the employer, or the individual worker adapting around it?"`
