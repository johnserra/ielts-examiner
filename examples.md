## Exchange 3: Candidate Gets It Right (Unscripted Precision, Self-Repair & Holding Ground)

**Callum:** Why do organizations often adopt software tools that end up creating more administrative work than they remove?

**Candidate:** Usually because the people buying the software aren't the people doing the data entry. A director sees a clean dashboard in a sales demo—sorry, a clean reporting view—and assumes the underlying records already exist in that shape. So the frontline team ends up re-keying invoices twice just to feed a tool that was supposed to save them an hour a day.

**Callum:** Right. Doesn't that just shift the blame onto poor implementation rather than the software itself? If the staff were trained properly, wouldn't the extra work disappear?

**Candidate:** Training helps when the problem is unfamiliarity, sure, but it doesn't fix mismatched incentives. If an older workflow only needed three fields and the new system demands twelve mandatory tags for analytics nobody reads, even a well-trained clerk spends four times longer per record. At that point the software isn't failing to be adopted—it's succeeding at shifting reporting labour downward.

**Callum:** If a tool creates extra labour at the bottom to produce visibility at the top, why don't senior managers see the productivity drop in their own numbers?

**Candidate:** Because the time cost is rarely measured on the same ledger. Dashboards track completed records per week, not unpaid overtime or delayed follow-ups, so the friction stays invisible until turnover climbs six months later.
